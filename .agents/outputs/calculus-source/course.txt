1.1 The tangent and velocity problems
The two questions that created calculus.
The tangent and velocity problems
Calculus began with two problems that turn out to be the same problem. The tangent
problem asks for the slope of a curve at a single point. The velocity problem asks for the
speed of a moving object at a single instant. Algebra cannot answer either directly,
because a slope needs two points and a speed needs two times.
Secant lines and average velocity
Between two points on the graph of f, the secant slope is the average rate of change:
msec = [f(x + h) − f(x)] / h.
If f(t) is position, this is average velocity over a time interval of length h.
From average to instantaneous
Shrink h. The secant lines swing toward a limiting line — the tangent line — and the
average velocities settle toward a limiting value — the instantaneous velocity. For s(t) =
16t2, the average velocity on [2, 2 + h] is 64 + 16h, which approaches 64 as h approaches
0. Making "approaches" precise is the job of the limit.
A real example
Around 450 BCE Zeno of Elea posed the arrow paradox: at any single instant a flying arrow
occupies a space exactly its own size, so at that instant it is not moving — and since time is
made of instants, it never moves. Calculus answers Zeno by defining velocity at an instant
not from the instant alone but as the limit of average velocities over shrinking intervals
around it. Newton and Leibniz each built the method in the late seventeenth century.

1.2 Limits
What a function approaches, whether or not it gets there.
Limits
The limit of f(x) as x approaches a is the value f(x) gets arbitrarily close to when x is
sufficiently close to a — but not equal to a. The value f(a) itself, or whether f is even
defined at a, is irrelevant.
Notation and one-sided limits
limx→a f(x) = L.
The one-sided limits limx→a− and limx→a+ approach from the left and right. The two-sided
limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal. If f(x) grows without
bound, we write lim f(x) = ∞ and the graph has a vertical asymptote.
The precise definition
Intuition is not enough for proofs. The ε–δ definition says limx→a f(x) = L if for every ε > 0
there is a δ > 0 such that
0 < |x − a| < δ ⇒ |f(x) − L| < ε.
However tight a tolerance ε you demand on the output, some tolerance δ on the input
guarantees it.
A real example
In 1734 Bishop George Berkeley attacked Newton's calculus in The Analyst, mocking its
vanishing increments as "ghosts of departed quantities": they were treated as nonzero
when dividing and as zero when discarded. The objection was sound, and it took more than
a century to answer. Augustin-Louis Cauchy in the 1820s and Karl Weierstrass later in the
century rebuilt calculus on the limit, and the ε–δ definition is Weierstrass's answer to
Berkeley.
1.3 Computing limits
The limit laws, algebraic tricks, and the squeeze theorem.
Computing limits
Limits obey arithmetic. If lim f(x) and lim g(x) both exist, the limit of a sum, difference,
product, or quotient is the sum, difference, product, or quotient of the limits — provided no
denominator limit is zero.
Direct substitution and indeterminate forms
For polynomials, rational functions (away from zeros of the denominator), roots, and the
other elementary functions, the limit is found by substituting x = a. When substitution
gives 0/0, the limit is indeterminate: factor and cancel, rationalize, or simplify first.
limx→3 (x2 − 9)/(x − 3) = limx→3 (x + 3) = 6.

The squeeze theorem
If g(x) ≤ f(x) ≤ h(x) near a and both g and h approach L, then f approaches L too. The
squeeze proves the most important trigonometric limit:
limx→0 sin x / x = 1.
Limits at infinity are handled by dividing by the highest power: the ratio of leading terms
decides a rational function's horizontal asymptote.
A real example
Archimedes, around 250 BCE, trapped π between the perimeters of polygons inscribed in
and circumscribed about a circle. Doubling the number of sides from 6 up to 96, he proved
3 10/71 < π < 3 1/7. The circle's perimeter is squeezed between two sequences closing in
on the same value — the squeeze theorem, two thousand years before it had a name.
1.4 Continuity
Functions whose graphs have no breaks.
Continuity
A function is continuous at a if its limit there equals its value there:
The definition
limx→a f(x) = f(a).
Three things are required: f(a) is defined, the limit exists, and the two agree.
Discontinuities are removable (a hole), jump (unequal one-sided limits), or infinite (a
vertical asymptote). Polynomials, rational functions on their domains, roots, exponentials,
logarithms, and trigonometric functions are continuous wherever they are defined, as are
sums, products, quotients, and compositions of them.
The intermediate value theorem
If f is continuous on [a, b] and N lies between f(a) and f(b), then f(c) = N for some c in (a,
b). A continuous function cannot skip values. In particular, a continuous function that
changes sign must have a zero — the basis of the bisection method, which halves the
search interval at each step.
f(a) < 0 < f(b) ⇒ f(c) = 0 for some c.
A real example
At every moment there are two diametrically opposite points on Earth's equator with
exactly the same temperature. Let T(θ) be the temperature at longitude θ and let g(θ) =
T(θ) − T(θ + 180°). Then g at the opposite point is −g, so g changes sign as θ runs halfway
around; by the intermediate value theorem it is zero somewhere. Continuity alone forces
the result, with no measurement at all.

1.5 The derivative
The instantaneous rate of change, defined by a limit.
The derivative
The derivative of f at x is the limit of the difference quotient — the slope of the tangent
line, the instantaneous rate of change:
The definition
f′(x) = limh→0 [f(x + h) − f(x)] / h.
Leibniz's notation dy/dx records it as a ratio of changes; Newton's dot notation survives in
physics for time derivatives. The second derivative f″ is the derivative of f′ — for position,
it is acceleration.
Differentiability
A function differentiable at a is continuous there, but not conversely. A derivative fails to
exist at a corner (|x| at 0), a cusp, a vertical tangent (the cube root at 0), or any
discontinuity. The tangent line at a is
y = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a).
A real example
Mathematicians long assumed continuous functions must be differentiable at most points.
In 1872 Karl Weierstrass presented a function — an infinite sum of cosine waves of
increasing frequency and decreasing height — that is continuous everywhere and
differentiable nowhere. Its graph is all corners. The shock established that continuity and
differentiability are genuinely different, and such functions later became the mathematics
of fractals and Brownian motion.
1.6 Differentiation rules
Computing derivatives without limits.
Differentiation rules
Applying the limit definition every time is impractical. A handful of rules, each proved once
from the definition, differentiate every algebraic function.
Power, sum, and constant rules
d/dx xn = n xn−1.
The power rule holds for every real exponent n. The derivative of a constant is 0, constants
factor out, and the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives.
Product and quotient rules
The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives:
(fg)′ = f′g + fg′, (f/g)′ = (f′g − fg′) / g2.

The quotient rule follows from the product rule applied to f · (1/g). Higher derivatives are
found by applying the rules repeatedly.
A real example
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz published the first account of the differential calculus in 1684, in
a six-page paper in the journal Acta Eruditorum titled "Nova Methodus pro Maximis et
Minimis." In it he stated the product rule in his own notation, d(xv) = x dv + v dx, along
with the quotient and power rules — and the notation dx, dy that students still use.
1.7 Derivatives of trigonometric and exponential functions
The transcendental functions, and why radians and e are natural.
Derivatives of trigonometric and exponential functions
From the limit sin x / x → 1 and the sum formula for sine, the derivatives of the
trigonometric functions follow:
Trigonometric derivatives
d/dx sin x = cos x, d/dx cos x = −sin x.
The quotient rule then gives d/dx tan x = sec2 x and the rest. These formulas hold only in
radians; in degrees each would carry a factor of π/180.
Exponential derivatives
Differentiating bx from the definition gives bx times a constant that depends on b. The
number e is the unique base for which that constant is 1:
d/dx ex = ex, d/dx bx = bx ln b.
The exponential ex is its own derivative — the reason it describes every quantity whose
rate of change is proportional to its size.
A real example
A mass on a spring oscillates as x(t) = A cos ωt. Its velocity x′(t) = −Aω sin ωt is zero at the
extremes of the motion and greatest as it passes through equilibrium; its acceleration x″(t)
= −ω2 x is always proportional and opposite to displacement. Christiaan Huygens
exploited this regular oscillation in the pendulum clock he built in 1656, which improved
timekeeping accuracy from minutes to seconds per day.

2.1 The chain rule
Differentiating compositions — the most used rule in calculus.
The chain rule
Most functions are compositions: sin(x2), e3x, √(1 + x4). The chain rule differentiates them
by multiplying rates of change.
The rule
d/dx f(g(x)) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x).
In Leibniz notation, if y depends on u and u depends on x,
dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx).
Differentiate the outer function leaving the inner alone, then multiply by the derivative of
the inner function.
Longer chains
For compositions of three or more functions the rule repeats: each layer contributes a
factor. Thus d/dx sin3(2x) = 3 sin2(2x) · cos(2x) · 2. Nearly every derivative beyond the
basic rules uses the chain rule, and its reverse — substitution — is the main integration
technique of Week 3.
A real example
Near sea level, air pressure falls about 1 inch of mercury for every 1,000 feet of altitude.
An airliner climbing at 2,000 feet per minute therefore sees cabin-outside pressure change
at dP/dt = (dP/dh)(dh/dt) ≈ (−1/1,000)(2,000) = −2 inches of mercury per minute.
Pressurization systems are designed around exactly this product of rates.
2.2 Implicit differentiation and inverse functions
Differentiating equations that are not solved for y.
Implicit differentiation and inverse functions
Curves such as x2 + y2 = 25 define y only implicitly. Implicit differentiation
differentiates both sides with respect to x, treating y as a function of x and applying the
chain rule to every y term, then solves for dy/dx.
The method
From x2 + y2 = 25: 2x + 2y y′ = 0, so

dy/dx = −x / y.
At (3, 4) the tangent slope is −3/4, perpendicular to the radius, as geometry requires.
Inverse functions and logarithms
Differentiating f(f−1(x)) = x gives the derivative of any inverse, and in particular:
d/dx ln x = 1/x, d/dx arctan x = 1/(1 + x2).
Logarithmic differentiation — take ln of both sides, then differentiate implicitly —
handles functions like xx whose variable appears in both base and exponent.
A real example
In 1638 René Descartes challenged Pierre de Fermat to find the tangent lines to the curve
x3 + y3 = 3axy, now called the folium of Descartes, expecting Fermat's method to fail.
Fermat solved it. Implicit differentiation now does it in two lines: 3x2 + 3y2y′ = 3a(y + xy′),
so y′ = (ay − x2)/(y2 − ax).
2.3 Related rates
When two changing quantities are linked by an equation.
Related rates
If two quantities are related by an equation and both change over time, their rates of
change are related by the derivative of that equation with respect to time.
The procedure
• Draw a diagram and name every changing quantity as a function of t.
• Write an equation relating them — geometry, the Pythagorean theorem, similar
triangles.
• Differentiate both sides with respect to t using the chain rule.
• Only then substitute the numbers for the instant in question.
x2 + y2 = z2 ⇒ x x′ + y y′ = z z′.
The classic error
Substituting values before differentiating turns variables into constants and destroys their
rates. A ladder's length is constant, so its rate is zero; the distances of its foot and top are
not, so they must remain variables until after differentiation.
A real example
A police radar gun measures the rate at which the distance to a car is changing, not the
car's speed. From z2 = x2 + d2, with d the officer's distance from the road, z′ = (x/z)x′ = v
cos θ. An officer standing off to the side therefore always reads low — the "cosine effect" —
and the error grows as the car approaches. The discrepancy always favors the driver.

2.4 Linear approximation and Newton's method
Using the tangent line to estimate and to solve.
Linear approximation and Newton's method
Near a point where f is differentiable, its graph is nearly straight. The tangent line — the
linearization — approximates f nearby:
Linear approximation
f(x) ≈ L(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a).
Thus √4.1 ≈ 2 + (1/4)(0.1) = 2.025 (true value 2.0248…). In differential notation, dy = f′(x)
dx estimates the change in y, and physics uses the same idea whenever it replaces sin θ by
θ for small angles.
Newton's method
To solve f(x) = 0, start at a guess xn, follow the tangent line to where it crosses the axis,
and repeat:
xn+1 = xn − f(xn) / f′(xn).
Near a simple root the number of correct digits roughly doubles with each step. The
method can fail when f′ is near zero or the starting guess is poor.
A real example
The 1999 video game Quake III Arena needed 1/√x millions of times a second to light its 3D
scenes. Its source code computed a rough initial guess by manipulating the bits of the
floating-point number, then applied a single step of Newton's method to refine it —
accurate to within about 0.2 percent and far faster than the hardware's division and square
root at the time.
2.5 Extreme values and the mean value theorem
Where maxima and minima can occur, and what derivatives guarantee.
Extreme values and the mean value theorem
The extreme value theorem guarantees that a continuous function on a closed interval
[a, b] attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum. The question is where.
Critical points
Fermat's theorem: if f has a local extremum at an interior point c and f′(c) exists, then f′(c)
= 0. So extrema occur only at critical points — where f′ is zero or undefined — or at
endpoints. The closed interval method evaluates f at all of them and picks the largest
and smallest.
f′(c) = 0 or f′(c) undefined.
The mean value theorem
If f is continuous on [a, b] and differentiable on (a, b), then at some interior point the
instantaneous rate equals the average rate:

f′(c) = [f(b) − f(a)] / (b − a).
Consequences: a function with zero derivative is constant, and a function with positive
derivative is increasing.
A real example
Average-speed cameras, used on British motorways, photograph a car at two points miles
apart and divide the distance by the elapsed time. They never measure instantaneous
speed. But if the average exceeds the limit, the mean value theorem guarantees that at
some instant between the cameras the speedometer read exactly that average — so the
driver was speeding at some moment, whether or not anyone saw it.
2.6 Curve sketching
Reading the shape of a graph from its derivatives.
Curve sketching
The first and second derivatives describe a graph's shape completely enough to sketch it
without plotting.
The first derivative test
f is increasing where f′ > 0 and decreasing where f′ < 0. At a critical point where f′ changes
from positive to negative there is a local maximum; from negative to positive, a local
minimum; no sign change, no extremum.
Concavity and inflection
f is concave up where f″ > 0 (bending upward, like a cup) and concave down where f″ <
0. A point where concavity changes is an inflection point. The second derivative test:
f′(c) = 0, f″(c) > 0 ⇒ local minimum at c.
A complete sketch combines domain, intercepts, symmetry, asymptotes, intervals of
increase, extrema, concavity, and inflection points.
A real example
In the early weeks of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, epidemiologists watched cumulative
case counts for an inflection point. While cumulative cases are concave up, daily new cases
— the derivative — are rising; the inflection point is where daily cases peak and begin to
fall. "Flattening the curve" meant reaching that inflection sooner and lower.
2.7 Optimization
Finding the best possible value of a quantity.
Optimization
Optimization problems ask for the largest or smallest value of a quantity — area, cost,
time, profit — subject to constraints. The derivative turns them into equation solving.

The procedure
• Identify the quantity Q to optimize and draw a diagram.
• Express Q in terms of one variable, using the constraint to eliminate the others.
• Find the domain, then the critical points where Q′ = 0.
• Confirm the optimum by the closed interval method or a derivative test.
Q′(x) = 0.
Economic interpretation
Profit is maximized where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, R′(x) = C′(x): the last
unit produced adds exactly as much revenue as cost. A fence of fixed length enclosing the
most area is a square; a can holding fixed volume with least material has height equal to its
diameter.
A real example
In 1662 Pierre de Fermat proposed that light travels between two points along the path
that takes the least time. Minimizing the travel time of a ray crossing from air into water —
setting the derivative of total time to zero — yields sin θ1 / v1 = sin θ2 / v2, exactly Snell's
law of refraction. An observed law of optics turned out to be the solution of an optimization
problem.

3.1 Antiderivatives
Running differentiation in reverse.
Antiderivatives
An antiderivative of f is a function F with F′ = f. Because constants have derivative zero,
antiderivatives come in families: if F is one, every other is F + C.
Basic formulas
Each derivative rule read backwards is an antiderivative rule:
∫ xn dx = xn+1 / (n + 1) + C, n ≠ −1.
• ∫ 1/x dx = ln|x| + C — the case n = −1;
• ∫ ex dx = ex + C;
• ∫ cos x dx = sin x + C and ∫ sin x dx = −cos x + C.
Initial value problems
A condition such as F(0) = 5 selects one member of the family by determining C. Given
acceleration, one antiderivative gives velocity and a second gives position, with the initial
velocity and initial position as the two constants.
A real example
Near Earth's surface a falling body has constant acceleration of about −32 feet per second
squared. Antidifferentiating once gives v(t) = −32t + v0; again gives s(t) = −16t2 + v0t +
s0. Galileo discovered from experiment that distance fallen grows with the square of time;
calculus derives his law in two lines from the single fact of constant acceleration.

Sigma notation
Summation formulas make exact limits possible: Σ i = n(n + 1)/2 and Σ i2 = n(n + 1)(2n +

integral computed nineteen centuries before calculus.

3.4 The fundamental theorem of calculus
Differentiation and integration are inverse processes.
The fundamental theorem of calculus
Riemann sums compute integrals slowly. The fundamental theorem of calculus
connects the two halves of the subject and turns integration into antidifferentiation.

3.5 Substitution
The chain rule run backwards.
Substitution
Many integrands are the derivative of a composition, f(g(x))g′(x). Substitution recognizes
the pattern and simplifies it by renaming the inner function.
The rule
∫ f(g(x)) g′(x) dx = ∫ f(u) du, u = g(x).
Choose u as an inner function whose derivative appears (up to a constant) as a factor. Thus
∫ 2x cos(x2) dx becomes ∫ cos u du = sin(x2) + C.
Definite integrals
For a definite integral, change the limits along with the variable:

A real example
The Mercator map, published in 1569, spaces lines of latitude so that compass bearings
appear as straight lines; the spacing at latitude φ is the integral of sec φ. Edward Wright
tabulated it in 1599 by adding up secants numerically. Around 1645 Henry Bond noticed
the table matched ln tan(π/4 + φ/2), and calculus later confirmed it: multiplying sec φ by
(sec φ + tan φ)/(sec φ + tan φ) sets up a substitution.

3.6 Integration by parts
The product rule run backwards.
Integration by parts
Integrating (uv)′ = u′v + uv′ and rearranging gives the second major technique, for
integrands that are products of unlike functions.
The formula
∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du.
Choose u to become simpler when differentiated and dv to be easy to integrate. For ∫ x ex
dx, take u = x and dv = ex dx: the result is x ex − ex + C.
Patterns
• Polynomial times exponential or trigonometric: let u be the polynomial and repeat as
needed.
• Logarithms and inverse trigonometric functions: let u be that function, dv = dx — this
gives ∫ ln x dx = x ln x − x + C.
• ex sin x: two applications return the original integral, which is then solved for
algebraically.
A real example
Integration by parts gives the Laplace transform its central property: the transform of a
derivative f′ equals s times the transform of f, minus f(0). This turns linear differential
equations into algebraic ones. Electrical and control engineers use it daily to analyze
circuits, feedback systems, and autopilots — each application a disguised integration by
parts.
3.7 Numerical integration
Computing integrals when no antiderivative can be found.
Numerical integration
Many integrals cannot be done by formula. Joseph Liouville proved in the 1830s that e−x²
— the bell curve — has no elementary antiderivative, yet its integrals are needed
everywhere in statistics. They are computed numerically.
The trapezoidal rule
Replace the curve on each subinterval by a straight line:
Tn = (Δx/2)[f(x0) + 2f(x1) + … + 2f(xn−1) + f(xn)].
The error shrinks in proportion to 1/n2: doubling n cuts it by about a factor of four.
Simpson's rule
Replace the curve on each pair of subintervals by a parabola (n even):
Sn = (Δx/3)[f(x0) + 4f(x1) + 2f(x2) + … + 4f(xn−1) + f(xn)].

A real example
Naval architects compute the areas and volumes of ship hulls from tables of offsets —
widths measured at evenly spaced stations along the hull. Simpson's rules have been the
standard tool for this for centuries: applied to the offsets they give the waterplane area,
the underwater volume, and hence the displacement and stability of a ship before it is

Integrating the gap between two functions.
Area between curves

If the region has a hole, each slice is a washer with area π(R2 − r2).
Cylindrical shells
Rotating about the y-axis, it is often easier to use thin cylindrical shells of radius x and

V = 2π ∫a

Disks slice perpendicular to the axis; shells slice parallel to it. Choose whichever gives the

A real example
In 1613 Johannes Kepler noticed that Austrian wine merchants priced barrels by pushing a
rod diagonally through the bung-hole and reading its length, whatever the barrel's shape.
Skeptical, he wrote Nova Stereometria Doliorum Vinariorum (1615), computing volumes of
solids of revolution by slicing them into infinitely many thin pieces — an early step toward
integral calculus, prompted by a wine purchase.
4.3 Differential equations
Equations whose unknown is a function.
Differential equations
A differential equation relates a function to its derivatives. It expresses a law of change;
solving it predicts the future from the present.
Exponential growth and decay
The simplest model says the rate of change is proportional to the amount present:
dy/dt = ky ⇒ y = y0 ekt.
It is solved by separation of variables: dy/y = k dt, integrate both sides, and
exponentiate.
Logistic growth and slope fields
Real populations are limited by a carrying capacity K:
dP/dt = rP(1 − P/K).
Growth is nearly exponential when P is small and slows to zero as P approaches K,
producing the S-shaped logistic curve. A slope field sketches solutions graphically when
no formula is available; Euler's method follows it numerically in small steps.
A real example
Newton's law of cooling says an object's temperature changes at a rate proportional to its
difference from the surroundings: dT/dt = −k(T − Ta). Its solution, T = Ta + (T0 − Ta)e−kt,
is used in forensic medicine: from two body-temperature readings taken some time apart,
investigators solve for k and then for the time at which the body was at a normal 98.6°F —
an estimate of the time of death.

4.4 L'Hôpital's rule and improper integrals
Indeterminate limits, and integrals over infinite intervals.
L'Hôpital's rule and improper integrals
Limits of the form 0/0 or ∞/∞ can be evaluated by differentiating numerator and
denominator separately.
L'Hôpital's rule
limx→a f(x)/g(x) = limx→a f′(x)/g′(x).
It shows that exponentials beat polynomials, which beat logarithms: lim xn/ex = 0 and lim
ln x / x = 0. Other indeterminate forms — 0·∞, ∞ − ∞, 1∞, 00 — are first rewritten as
quotients. The rule is actually Johann Bernoulli's; the Marquis de L'Hôpital paid for his
results and published them in the first calculus textbook in 1696.
Improper integrals
An integral over an infinite interval, or of a function with a vertical asymptote, is defined as
a limit:
∫1
∞ f(x) dx = limt→∞ ∫1
t f(x) dx.
It converges if the limit is finite. The benchmark: ∫1
∞ 1/xp dx converges exactly when p >
1.
A real example
In 1641 Evangelista Torricelli rotated y = 1/x for x ≥ 1 about the x-axis. The resulting
infinitely long horn — now called Gabriel's horn — has volume π∫1
∞ 1/x2 dx = π, yet its

4.5 Infinite series
Adding infinitely many numbers.
Infinite series
An infinite series Σ an is the limit of its partial sums SN = a1 + … + aN. It converges if
that limit exists and diverges otherwise.
Basic results
• Divergence test: if an does not approach 0, the series diverges. The converse is false.
• Geometric series: Σ arn converges to a/(1 − r) when |r| < 1.
• p-series: Σ 1/np converges exactly when p > 1 — the integral test links it to Lecture

Σn=1
∞ 1/n = ∞.

Convergence tests
The comparison tests measure a series against a known one. The ratio test examines lim
|an+1/an|: less than 1 converges, greater than 1 diverges. The alternating series test says
terms that alternate in sign and decrease to zero give a convergent series, as with 1 − 1/2
+ 1/3 − … = ln 2.
A real example
Around 1350 Nicole Oresme proved the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … diverges by
grouping its terms into blocks each exceeding 1/2. The series Σ 1/n2 converges, but to
what resisted the best mathematicians of Europe until 1734, when Leonhard Euler showed
its sum is π2/6 — a result that made his reputation. It is still called the Basel problem.
4.6 Power series
Infinite polynomials.
Power series
A power series is a series with a variable: an infinite polynomial centered at a.
Radius of convergence
Σn=0
∞ cn (x − a)n.
Every power series has a radius of convergence R: it converges for |x − a| < R and
diverges for |x − a| > R, with the endpoints checked separately. The ratio test usually finds
R. The model example is the geometric series 1/(1 − x) = 1 + x + x2 + …, with R = 1.
Calculus with power series
Inside its interval of convergence a power series can be differentiated and integrated term
by term, like a polynomial. Integrating 1/(1 + x2) = 1 − x2 + x4 − … gives
arctan x = x − x3/3 + x5/5 − ….
Power series thus produce new functions and new representations of old ones.
A real example
Setting x = 1 in the arctangent series gives π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …. Europeans
credit James Gregory and Leibniz, who found it in the 1670s, but Madhava of
Sangamagrama and the Kerala school in southern India had it around 1400, along with
series for sine and cosine and correction terms to speed convergence — an early mastery
of infinite series.
4.7 Taylor series
Every smooth function as a polynomial of infinite degree.
Taylor series
If f has a power series at a, its coefficients are forced by its derivatives: cn = f(n)(a)/n!. The
resulting Taylor series is

The series
f(x) = Σn=0
∞ f(n)(a) (x − a)n / n!.
Centered at 0 it is called a Maclaurin series. The essential examples:
• ex = 1 + x + x2/2! + x3/3! + …, for all x;
• sin x = x − x3/3! + x5/5! − …;
• cos x = 1 − x2/2! + x4/4! − ….
Taylor polynomials and error
Truncating after degree n gives the Taylor polynomial Tn, the best polynomial
approximation of f near a. Taylor's inequality bounds the error by M|x − a|n+1/(n + 1)!,
where M bounds |f(n+1)|. Substituting ix into ex and comparing with the sine and cosine
series gives Euler's formula eix = cos x + i sin x.
A real example
Einstein's relativistic kinetic energy is mc2(1/√(1 − v2/c2) − 1). Expanding in a Taylor
series in v/c gives ½mv2 + (3/8)mv4/c2 + …. The first term is Newton's kinetic energy; the
rest are corrections too small to notice at everyday speeds. Taylor series show precisely
why Newtonian physics works so well — and where it stops working.
4.8 Capstone synthesis
One idea behind everything: the local determines the global.
Capstone synthesis
Step back, and the entire course reduces to a single sentence: calculus studies change by
taking limits, and the derivative and the integral are inverse operations.
The one theorem
∫a
b f′(x) dx = f(b) − f(a).
Everything you have learned elaborates this statement:
• Limits make instantaneous rates precise (Week 1).
• Derivatives measure local change and locate optima (Week 2).
• Integrals add up local change into global totals (Week 3).
• Differential equations and series predict and approximate from local information (Week
4).
Where calculus goes next
Multivariable calculus extends these ideas to functions of several variables — partial
derivatives, multiple integrals, and the theorems of Green, Gauss, and Stokes, which
generalize the fundamental theorem. Differential equations, probability, and physics rest
on everything here.

A real example
Konstantin Tsiolkovsky derived the rocket equation in 1903. Conservation of momentum
gives a differential relation: a rocket ejecting mass dm at exhaust speed ve gains speed dv
= −ve dm/m. Integrating from initial mass m0 to final mass mf gives Δv = ve ln(m0/mf).
Rates, a differential equation, an integral, and a logarithm — the whole course — explain
why rockets are mostly fuel.
